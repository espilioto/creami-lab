## Sugars & Sweeteners

### The Role of Sugar in Ice Cream

Sugar does three things, not one:
1. **Sweetness** — the obvious role
2. **Freezing point depression (FPD)** — without sugar, ice cream is a solid block of ice. Sugar reduces the freezing point so the mix becomes scoopable ice cream instead of a frozen rock
3. **Solids** — sugar contributes essential solids. Without enough solids, the mix is watery with poor mouthfeel

The more sugar you add, the more it dulls other flavors — both dairy and sugar mask flavor perception.

### Sucrose (Table Sugar)

The baseline everything else is measured against:
- **POD = 100** (sweetening power — relative sweetness)
- **PAC = 100** (anti-freezing power — freezing point depression)
- 4 calories per gram
- Naturally occurring in plants, fruits, vegetables, nuts. Commercially from sugar cane and sugar beets

**Usage ranges:**
- American hard-churned ice cream: **13-20%** sugar content
- Gelato: **15-23%** (higher sugar compensates for lower fat — doesn't taste overly sweet because less fat = less flavor lingering)
- Sherbets and low-fat: even higher sugar to compensate for lost solids

In standard homemade ice cream, sucrose usually accounts for 100% of added sugar (excluding lactose from dairy). You can make excellent ice cream with sucrose alone — other sugars become important when you get into the scientific side.

**If ice cream is too hard from the freezer:** the simplest fix is to add more sugar to increase FPD.

### Dextrose (Corn Sugar / Brewer's Sugar)

A simple sugar from corn or wheat. Called "corn sugar" in North America, "brewer's sugar" in UK. Essentially D-glucose — almost identical to glucose.

Two forms: monohydrate (one crystallized water molecule) and anhydrous (zero). Both work the same in ice cream.

- **POD = 70** (70-80% the sweetness of sucrose)
- **PAC = ~190** (almost TWICE the FPD of sucrose — this is the key number)

**Why use it:**
- Makes ice cream softer straight from freezer, easier to scoop
- If ice cream tastes right but is too sweet: replace 20-25% of sucrose with dextrose — keeps the same texture but drops perceived sweetness
- **Creami advantage:** Ice cream made with dextrose doesn't go rock hard when put back in the freezer after spinning. Sucrose-only ice cream at -13C serving temp goes rock solid and must be re-spun. Dextrose stays soft enough to just take out and eat
- Essential for Ninja Creami/Swirl — if you make ice cream too hard, the machine struggles to process it

**Usage:**
- **Starting ratio: 75% sucrose / 25% dextrose** — adjust from there
- Can use **up to 50% dextrose** (replacing sucrose) and still keep a recipe balanced
- Great for chocolate ice cream — chocolate always freezes harder, dextrose counteracts this

**Substitution:** If dextrose is unavailable, sub glucose directly 1:1 — powdered glucose, liquid glucose, or glucose syrup all work.

**Where to buy:** NOT in supermarkets. Amazon, brewery shops, wine-making shops. ~2kg bags, super cheap, lasts a year.

### Erythritol

A sugar alcohol (polyol) made by fermenting glucose with yeast. Found naturally in some fruits and fermented foods.

- **0.2 cal/g** — effectively zero-calorie
- **Does not raise blood sugar or insulin** — safe for diabetics
- Counters bacteria that cause tooth decay (unlike sugar which promotes it)
- Relatively well-tolerated digestively compared to other sugar alcohols
- **POD ~75** (60-70% sweetness of sucrose)
- **PAC = 290-320** — almost THREE TIMES the FPD of sucrose

**Three critical problems for ice cream:**

1. **Recrystallization.** Erythritol recrystallizes when subjected to freezing temperatures. Ice cream stays smooth for about **30 minutes**, then sugar alcohol granules recrystallize → very grainy, unpleasant, borderline inedible mouthfeel.

2. **Extreme FPD.** PAC of 290-320 means you can only use a **maximum of 1/3** the amount you'd use of sugar. If you substitute erythritol 1:1 for sugar, **your ice cream will not freeze at all** — it stays liquid in the freezer permanently. The math simply doesn't work at equal weights.

3. **Ultra-cold effect.** Even when it does freeze, the ice cream comes out too cold — unpleasantly cold on the tongue. This is the sensation you get at scoop shops that use erythritol poorly.

4. **Lost solids.** Because you can only use 1/3 the amount, you lose essential solids that sugar normally provides. Sugar is a major source of solids in ice cream. Those lost solids must be replaced from somewhere, making recipe balancing very difficult.

**Nick's verdict:** Leave erythritol for coffee, tea, or baked goods. Social media influencers use it to reduce calories but don't understand what they're doing — the ice cream only lasts 5 minutes before turning to junk in the freezer. Use xylitol or allulose instead.

**Safety (as of 2024):** FDA, European Food Safety Authority, WHO all consider it safe. Recent Cleveland Clinic studies raised concerns about blood clots and heart disease — not conclusive. Can have digestive side effects in excess.

### Invert Sugars

Options (all essentially very similar for ice cream purposes): corn syrup (dark or light), straight glucose syrup, honey, maple syrup, designated invert sugar, golden syrup.

**What they do:** Create a unique texture and mouthfeel in ice cream that is basically impossible to mimic without using them. Use in the amounts specified in recipes.

## Stabilizers

### What Stabilizers Do

Stabilizers prevent ice crystal growth, improve longevity, enhance texture, maintain structure during temperature fluctuations. Specifically:

- **Reduce ice crystal size** — good ice cream has lots of tiny ice crystals so small your tongue can't pick them out (= creamy). Bad ice cream has huge crystals you can taste and feel. Stabilizers keep crystals small, especially during freeze-thaw cycles in frost-free freezers
- **Reduce air bubble size** — more viscous base = smaller, more evenly distributed air bubbles = smoother texture
- **Improve smoothness and creaminess** — directly enhance mouthfeel
- **Slow melt rate** — ice cream melts more slowly and evenly
- **Control free water** — prevent free water from forming large ice crystals
- **Aid overrun** — certain stabilizers can increase overrun slightly (lighter ice cream)

**Natural vs "E-numbers":** Almost all ice cream stabilizers are natural — from beans, seaweed, plants, fruit. E-numbers are just regulatory classification labels, not indicators of danger. E412 = guar gum (from beans). E410 = locust bean gum (from carob tree seeds).

### General Rules

- **Dosage: 0.1% to 0.5%** of total base weight, depending on recipe, fat%, sugar%
- Even **0.25-0.5g too much** = the difference between nice texture and super gummy/slimy/unpleasant
- **Must use precision scales** — teaspoons are too inaccurate for stabilizers. Jeweler/micro scales cost <$10 on Amazon
- **Dispersion is critical:** ALWAYS mix stabilizer into dry solids (powders, sugars) first and stir thoroughly. If clumps hit the liquid, they will NOT break up — they stay as clumps and do nothing. They might break apart during churning and leave powder spots
- **Combining stabilizers works better than any single one** — synergistic effects mean combinations outperform individuals
- When using hot-process stabilizers, heat bases to **85C** for best results
- Account for water evaporation during long heating times
- **Age the base 4-24 hours** after mixing — allows full hydration, fat crystallization, protein hydration. Significantly improves body and texture

### When You Need Gels vs Not

- **Low-fat / no-fat ice cream / sorbet → YES, gel needed.** No fat = need gel to create body and the impression of creamy mouthfeel. Think of gel as a way to mimic what fat does
- **High-fat dairy ice cream → NO gel needed.** Will over-thicken, struggle to churn, get low overrun, likely go hard
- **Kappa or Iota carrageenan** create gels that mimic high-fat mouthfeel in low-fat bases. Lambda carrageenan thickens without gelling — use in medium-to-high fat ice cream

### Individual Stabilizers

#### Guar Gum (E412)
- From seeds of the guar plant (India/Pakistan). Seeds dehusked, ground, gum extracted. Off-white powder
- A galactomannan polysaccharide (galactose + mannose). Soluble fiber
- **Absorbs up to 8x more water than cornstarch** — a little goes a long way
- **Dosage: 0.1% to 0.3%** of total base weight — start low
- **Cold-process stabilizer** — optimal hydration at 50-60C, but works at 10C (just takes longer)
- Breaks down after **80C for 5 minutes** — don't overheat
- Creates smooth creamy texture, reduces ice crystal formation
- Especially useful in **low-fat or non-dairy** ice creams — can mimic dairy fat texture
- Adds viscosity → smaller air bubbles during churning
- Creates a **slightly stretchy texture** — delicious in homemade ice cream; low overrun of home machines benefits from guar
- **Best paired with locust bean gum** — synergistic ice crystal reduction far exceeds either alone
- Some people can taste guar gum (a "beanie" flavor from the source plant)
- Acts as a **prebiotic fiber** — feeds probiotic bacteria. In the 1980s people ate it by the teaspoon for weight loss and blocked their esophagus — no longer used for weight loss

#### Xanthan Gum (E415)
- Polysaccharide from fermenting corn/soy/wheat with *Xanthomonas campestris* bacteria. The bacteria produce a polysaccharide capsule (goo/slime). Treated with alcohol to kill bacteria, dried, crushed into white powder
- **Cold-process stabilizer** — hydrates in cold liquids, no heating required. Easiest standalone stabilizer for beginners
- Triple function: **stabilizer + emulsifier + mouthfeel improver**
- **Dosage: start at 0.1%** of base weight. Very powerful — a little goes a long way
- **Best added at cooler stage of production.** If added while base is on the hob, thickens very quickly → resulting ice cream will be **slightly slimy to taste**. This is the most common mistake
- Mix with sugar first to prevent clumping
- Cheap, widely available (Amazon, supermarkets)
- Some people react with bloating/IBS symptoms — try a different stabilizer if so
- Better for melt resistance than guar gum

#### Locust Bean Gum (LBG, E410)
- From seeds of the carob tree (Mediterranean regions). Also called carob gum. Seeds dried, roasted, ground to fine powder. Soluble fiber
- **Has one of the BEST ice crystal reduction capabilities** of any stabilizer — this is its superpower
- **Needs heat to hydrate: 80-85C** (hot-process only)
- **Poor gelling ability on its own** — this is actually desirable when used alone (no unwanted gel textures)
- If used alone: can cause **"wheying off"** — whey/clear liquid seeps out when ice cream melts. Partner stabilizers prevent this
- **Best partner: guar gum** — the classic pairing. Also excellent with carrageenan
- **Dosage: 0.1% to 0.3%** — 0.3% is already quite a lot
- Particularly beneficial for cheap machines (slower churn = bigger crystals = LBG helps more) and for re-freezing ice cream
- If sensitive to LBG, substitute with **Tara gum**

#### Carrageenan
- From **red seaweed**, harvested from coasts of Indonesia, Philippines, Chile. Extracted using hot alkali solution at ~100C

**Three types — different functions:**

| Type | Gel? | Use For |
|------|------|---------|
| **Kappa** | Strong, rigid gel | Low-fat ice cream, dairy products. Stiff, slightly brittle gel. Gel mimics fat mouthfeel |
| **Iota** | Soft, flexible gel | Custards, puddings. Soft elastic gel |
| **Lambda** | No gel — thickener only | High-fat ice cream where gelling is undesirable. Slows melt, holds fat+water longer |

- In **low-fat ice cream:** use Kappa or Iota — gel mimics the mouthfeel of high-fat
- In **medium-to-high fat:** use Lambda — thickens without gelling. A gel in high-fat ice cream creates slimy mouthfeel
- **Dosage: start at 0.1%** — use in moderation, always as part of a blend (not alone)
- Generally safe. Some studies suggest possible inflammation in the digestive tract for some people — context: red meat, refined grains, soda, and fried foods also cause inflammation

#### CMC (Carboxymethyl Cellulose, E466)
- Cellulose extracted mainly from **wood fibers**, then multi-stage chemical process (alkalization → etherification → neutralization → purification/drying)
- **Dosage: 0.2-0.5%**. Needs **20+ minutes at temperature** (above 75C) for proper hydration
- Holds free water, stabilizes fat/water emulsion, improves mouthfeel, aids overrun (higher than most stabilizers)
- **Big downside: quite a strong taste.** Light-flavored ice creams (strawberry, vanilla) will have CMC flavor overpower them. Can make ice cream taste fake/artificial
- Can create **pasty texture** if overused
- **Often combined with GMS.** Cheaper brands use CMC + GMS because both available in bulk at low cost
- Pushes the boundaries of "natural" — multi-stage chemical extraction from wood

#### GMS (Glycerol Monostearate, E471)
- Synthetic compound from glycerol + stearic acid (both can be natural or synthetic). Glycerol from vegetable oil or animal fats; stearic acid from cocoa butter, coconut oil, or palm oil
- **More emulsifier than stabilizer.** Can stabilize on its own but not very well
- Helps fats and water combine for smoother base → smoother mouthfeel. Increases viscosity → shrinks air bubble size
- **Is it better than lecithin as an emulsifier? No.** (Nick's direct assessment)
- When combined with CMC: reasonably good results
- Essential for **vegan recipes** — stabilizes plant fats (coconut oil) and ensures emulsification with nut milks
- **Starting ratio with CMC: 60% CMC to 30% GMS** (rest = other stabilizers)

#### Tara Gum
- From pods of the Tara tree (Peru, South America). Processed similarly to LBG (dried, ground, filtered)
- Has both **stabilizing and emulsifying/thickening properties** — multi-function
- **One of the best standalone stabilizers** — described as a "jack of all trades." Does most things other stabilizers do, just not quite as well at each individual function
- Ranked higher than xanthan and guar as a standalone
- Increases viscosity (creaminess, mouthfeel), prevents water/fat separation, reduces ice crystal formation, helps overrun
- **Dosage: 0.1% upwards.** Needs heat: 75-85C, hold 15-20 minutes
- **Quite expensive** and difficult to find, especially in North America
- Good substitute for LBG if you're sensitive to it
- Nick's personal favorite standalone stabilizer

#### Starches (Tapioca & Corn)

| | Tapioca Starch | Cornstarch |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Cassava root | Corn kernel endosperm |
| Flavor | **No flavor** | **Strong, distinct flavor** — "absolute BS" that it has no flavor |
| Finish | Very glossy/clear | Opaque |
| Heat tolerance | Good at lower temps, **breaks down with continual heating** | **Handles continual high heat well** |
| Gluten | Free | Free |

- Both popular in Asia, easily accessible there
- Require **much more** than other stabilizers (they're weaker)
- Tapioca is generally better for ice cream due to no flavor and clear finish
- If you go too far with cornstarch: powdery, unpleasant taste
- **Not as effective** at controlling ice crystal formation and improving mouthfeel as gums

#### Other Stabilizing Agents
- **Fatty Acids (E471, mono/diglycerides):** Primarily emulsifier. Helps fats and water combine into smoother base. Increases viscosity → smaller air bubbles
- **Sodium Alginate (E401):** From brown ocean kelp. Dissolves in cold water, hydrates better around 70C. Contributes to better texture/body than some gums
- **Agar Agar (E406):** Natural vegetable gelatin counterpart — vegan-friendly. Thickening agent. Needs boiling. Creates brittle gel — use carefully
- **Gelatin:** Stabilizes free water, forms gel, prevents large ice crystals. Creates smoother mouthfeel. If solids are too low, gelatin can make ice cream feel much colder than it should
- **Pectin:** Naturally occurring in fruit. Bonds water, thickens, creates gel (like in jam). Powdered pectin can go gritty in the freezer over time
- **Milk Powder:** "A great stabilizer" — bonds free water, increases solids. Borderline essential for dairy ice creams to achieve adequate MSNF. Biggest challenge for vegan recipes that need alternative solid sources
- **Sugar:** To a degree, sugar stabilizes free water (like making syrup). Increasing sugar content is an approach if avoiding other stabilizers

### Stabilizer Combinations (Blends)

**Combination 1 — Most Common Commercial Blend:**
- **LBG : Guar : Lambda Carrageenan = 4:2:1**
- LBG = incredible ice crystal suppression (during churn AND in freezer)
- Guar = synergistic with LBG for even better crystal reduction + better viscosity (thickens free water)
- Lambda carrageenan = slows melt, holds fat and water molecules longer (longevity)

**Combination 2 — With Emulsifier:**
- **Lecithin : LBG : Guar : Lambda Carrageenan = 4:4:2:1**
- Emulsifier bonds fat and water into stable emulsion
- Sunflower lecithin and soy lecithin are better emulsifiers than egg yolk lecithin

**Combination 3 — Gelatin + Xanthan:**
- Gelatin = most important when frozen/hard — stops free water movement during freeze-thaw
- Xanthan = strongest effect when melting — slows melt as you eat

**Synergistic gel pairs** (form gels when combined that neither forms alone):
- LBG + Xanthan gum
- LBG + Kappa carrageenan
- CMC + Guar gum
- CMC + All carrageenans

**Stabilizers that form gels alone:**
- LBG (weak gel)
- Kappa carrageenan (stiff brittle gel)
- Iota carrageenan (soft elastic gel)
- Sodium alginate (rigid hard brittle gel)
- Gelatin (brittle gel)
- Pectin (brittle but sticky gel)

**Real-world example — Ben & Jerry's Tonight Dough ingredients:** soy lecithin, guar gum, carrageenan. Confirms major brands use these blends.

### Egg Yolks as Stabilizer/Emulsifier

- **Think of egg yolks as an emulsifier, not a stabilizer** — they're just not very good at long-term stabilization
- The lecithin in yolks bonds water and fat molecules into a stable emulsion (= custard). Heating coagulates the protein, further thickening
- A large egg: ~70-78 cal, 7g protein, 5g fat, 1.6g saturated fat
- **Only the yolk matters** — it has the lecithin + most of the fat. The white does nothing for ice cream
- The eggy taste IS the defining flavor of French custard ice cream. When other flavors are added (cocoa, mint, vanilla), the egg taste gets masked — at which point, why use eggs?
- **Modern stabilizers are more effective** than eggs without the egg flavor
- American ice cream traditionally uses NO eggs — relies on stabilizers
- ~90% of online recipe bloggers use eggs and most don't understand why — it's tradition/habit
- Haagen-Dazs and Ben & Jerry's used to use only egg yolks. Fine for short-term custard bases but eggs are not good at long-term stabilization — they're changing their approach

## Recipe Balancing

### Key Ranges

| Parameter | Ice Cream | Gelato | Low-Fat | Sorbet |
|-----------|-----------|--------|---------|--------|
| Fat % | 10-24% | 6-12% | <5% | 0-2% |
| Sugar % | 13-20% | 15-23% | Varies | 22-28% |
| Total Solids % | Late 30s - mid 40s | 36-43% | Late 20s (needs intervention) | 28-34% |
| Water % | Late 40s - early 60s | — | — | — |
| MSNF % | 5%+ (essential for mouthfeel) | 11-12% | — | — |

### The Most Important Metric: Freezing Point

**Freezing point is MORE important than sugar percentage.** They often go hand-in-hand but not always. Different sugars (dextrose, erythritol, xylitol, allulose) each affect freezing point differently at the same weight. When you add cream, milk, SMP, different sugars — they all change the freezing point.

**Serving temperature determines scoopability:**
- **-7C** → likely won't freeze solid at all in a home freezer
- **-10C** → need to temper from -18C freezer before eating
- **-13C** → Ninja Creami typical serving temp. Sucrose-only goes rock hard when put back in freezer

### Balancing for Ninja Creami

- **Balancing small batches (550-680ml) is harder** than at 1L/1kg scale. The Deluxe 750ml is easier than the standard 550ml
- **Cold-process stabilizers preferred** — just easier (no cooking, no heating, just blend and freeze)
- Guar gum tip: mix 1/8 tsp guar with 1 tsp sugar before adding to liquid — prevents lumps. The 1 tsp sugar makes no difference to the recipe
- Microwave 30 seconds to warm base just enough to dissolve thick ingredients (dulce de leche, honey) into dairy — not superheating

**Dextrose advantage in Creami:** If there's already lots of sugar in an ingredient (like arequipa with 40-45% sugar), adding table sugar can't get the balance right — too much sugar, too many solids, but freezing point not high enough. Dextrose solves this: less sweet but almost double the PAC. And it doesn't re-harden when put back in freezer.

**Example recipe (550g Creami pint):**
- 167g 2% milk, 205g 33% cream, 150g arequipa, 39g dextrose, 0.8g salt, guar gum
- Analysis: 61% water, 38% solids, 5% MSNF, 18% sugar, 13.3% fat

### Gelato vs Ice Cream

| | Gelato | Ice Cream |
|---|---|---|
| Fat | 6-12% | 10-24% |
| Sugar | 15-23% | 13-20% |
| Overrun (air) | Less (denser) | More (lighter) |
| Serving temp | Warmer (-10 to -14C) | Colder (-12 to -16C) |
| Texture | Dense, slight chew | Lighter, creamier |
| Stabilizers | Heavier use (compensates for low fat) | Less needed (fat does much of the work) |

Higher fat makes taste linger longer. Gelato taste ends sooner (less fat). At home, you can't really control overrun, so the main difference is fat/sugar balance.

### Why Homemade Ice Cream Gets Icy

Two causes:
1. **Unbalanced recipe** — too much free water → forms large ice crystals
2. **Frost-free freezer** — has a built-in freeze-thaw cycle. Ice cream partially melts overnight, then refreezes. Each cycle grows ice crystals larger until texture is ruined. Stabilizers counteract this

### Solids Are the Key to Creaminess

The secret to creamy texture is the **amount of solids** — milk solids, milk fat, dairy fat, butter fat. These have a huge impact on creamy mouthfeel. But the recipe must be balanced — solids alone won't save an unbalanced recipe.

### No Universal Base Recipe

"There is no catch-all. There is no one recipe that works for everything." Different ingredients (chocolate vs vanilla, various fruits) have different fat, sugar, and water contents that require unique balancing. This is why calculators are essential.

## Zero-Fat & High-Protein Formulation

### Zero-Fat Challenges

Gelato starts around 6% fat. Below 5% is low-fat territory. Making these is **vastly more complicated** than standard ice cream.

**Problem 1 — Mouthfeel:** Butterfat is the biggest contributor to mouthfeel. No cream = no luxury mouthfeel or creamy texture.

**Problem 2 — Lost solids:** Without cream, solids drop into the late 20s%. Standard high-fat ice cream has solids in the 38-45% range. The gap must be filled.

**Problem 3 — Sugar substitutes make it worse:** Allulose requires less volume than sugar for the same effect, so you lose even more solids. Result: watered-down, ice-heavy frozen dessert.

**Solutions:**
- **Maltodextrin** — water-soluble carbohydrate powder (like a starch). Filler that boosts solids without the calories of sugar
- **Protein sources** — whey protein, SMP (but SMP comes with lots of lactose = calories, and too much SMP overrides flavor)
- **Stabilizer blend** with emulsifier — needed to create a cohesive mix of limited fats and water
- **Inulin** — for plant-based, mimics mouthfeel that milk proteins provide
- **Tiny amount of real sugar** (<1g per 100g mix) — creates much more natural flavor without meaningful calories

**Zero Fat Vanilla recipe:**
- 630g skim milk (set 30g aside for post-chill), 100g SMP, 30g WPC80, 150g allulose, 40g maltodextrin, 30g inulin, 4g stabilizers, 2g lecithin, 10g vanilla extract, 34g water

### High Protein Formulation

**Commercial protein ice cream is mostly air.** Halo Top tub: 269g. Ben & Jerry's tub: 423g. You're getting 40% less ice cream because it's pumped with air.

**Overrun ranges and quality:**
- **25-40% overrun** = ultra-high quality texture (the sweet spot)
- Above 40% = starts to taste like frozen dessert, not ice cream
- US legal limit: 100% overrun (double volume with air and still call it "ice cream")
- Home machines: typically 15-20% overrun. Higher-quality home machines: up to 40% with the right recipe

**1L base + 40% overrun = 1.4L final. 1L base + 100% overrun = 2L** — this is how commercial brands stretch product.

**High protein chocolate recipe (zero sugar):**
- 600g 2% milk, 250g 33% cream, 115g xylitol, 60g chocolate protein powder, 50g SMP, 15g cocoa (20-22% fat), 1.5g guar gum
- Process: warm to 40-50C (NOT 85C — cold-process stabilizer). Just enough for xylitol to dissolve, cocoa to blend, powders to hydrate. Stir, chill overnight, churn
- Result: just over **9g protein per 100g** — nearly double Halo Top's 7g per 180ml (which is mostly air)

**Warnings:**
- Don't add too much protein — creates fudge-thickness base that won't churn
- Switching from chocolate to vanilla requires **complete rebalancing** — cocoa powder (20-22% fat) significantly affects the recipe
- SMP is loaded with protein — don't forget it as a protein source
- Plant-based protein powders may not dissolve well → grainy texture
- Use high-quality cocoa — cheap cocoa tastes bad and may not dissolve properly

## Chocolate & Cocoa

### Cocoa Powder Selection

**Fat content categories:**
| Category | Fat% | Examples | Notes |
|----------|------|---------|-------|
| High fat | 22-24% | Callebaut, IRCA | Richest flavor, most body |
| Medium fat | 16-17% | Severn Hills | Unusual range, milky character |
| Low fat | 10-12% | deZaan True Dark | More intense, less body |
| Ultra low | ~1% or less | Black cocoa | Tastes disgusting unless heavily boiled/cooked |

**Key insight: fat content does NOT determine milk vs dark chocolate flavor.** Color and flavor depth are determined primarily by **roasting process, bean origin, and degree of alkalization** (Dutch processing) — not just fat percentage.

**Tested cocoa powders:**
- **Severn Hills** — milky taste, lighter color. Best for: exact Haagen-Dazs Belgian Chocolate clone ("no substitute, use this one")
- **Hershey's Dark** — dissolved easily but very flat flavor, lacks depth. Not recommended for ice cream
- **Rodelle** (Costco) — darker, deeper, complex. Good middle ground
- **Ghirardelli Dutched** — great taste, darker, heavier. Complex flavors. Dutch processed

**Critical warning:** Drinking chocolate is NOT cocoa powder — it's mostly sugar. Using it to flavor ice cream makes balancing essentially impossible.

### Cocoa Powder vs Physical Chocolate

**Misconception:** People think premium chocolate ice cream must use physical chocolate and cocoa powder is "cheap." This is wrong. Luxury brands most likely use cocoa powder or cocoa+chocolate combinations, not just physical chocolate.

**Why:** Adding more physical chocolate increases solids significantly each time — can become a problem. Cocoa powder gives chocolate flavor without as much solids impact.

**Comparable recipes (same approximate fat/sugar/solids):**

Cocoa version: 500g whole milk, 400g cream @49%, 220g sugar, 54g SMP, 50g cocoa @21% fat → 17.9% fat, 9.3% MSNF, 18% sugar, 48% solids

Chocolate version: 480g milk, 300g cream @49%, 90g sugar, 75g chocolate @55-56%, 40g SMP → 18.8% fat, 9.6% MSNF, 17.8% sugar, 42.5% solids

### How Cocoa Powder Is Made

1. Cocoa beans fermented (warm weather, forest regions)
2. Cracked, dried, oven-roasted
3. Machined into semi-liquid paste
4. Compressed to remove cocoa butter (fat)
5. Remainder dried → cocoa powder

### Why Chocolate Ice Cream Is Hard

One of the hardest "simple" ice creams to make. Many internet recipes are too low in sugars, too high in fats, or generally just bad. A gelateria in Oxford couldn't get chocolate gelato right — struggled so badly they stopped selling it. All chocolates and cocoa powders differ in cocoa solids, milk, sugar, and fat content — all affect the result differently.

## Dairy Techniques

### High-Fat Cream via Evaporation

**Never add butter to increase cream fat.** Butter cannot re-emulsify into cream without an emulsifier. It separates when chilled, produces disgusting slimy mouthfeel in ice cream.

**The correct method:** Evaporate off excess water — the same process dairies use.

**Procedure:**
1. Weigh starting cream
2. Calculate target weight (formula below)
3. Bring to very low simmer — **NEVER boil** (boiling scalds cream, changes taste permanently)
4. Stir/agitate continuously
5. Monitor weight periodically (weigh pan or pour into container)
6. Cool and refrigerate overnight when target reached

**Formula:**
```
Fat weight = starting cream weight x cream fat%
Target weight = fat weight / target fat%
Water to remove = starting weight - target weight
```

**Example:** 500g of 33% cream → 50% fat cream:
- Fat = 0.33 x 500 = 165g
- Target = 165 / 0.50 = 330g
- Remove 170g water

**Reference table — starting weight needed to make 250g of 50% cream:**
| Starting fat% | Weight needed | Approx time |
|--------------|--------------|-------------|
| 18% | 695g | ~2 hours |
| 30% | 417g | — |
| 33% | 379g | ~45 min |
| 36% | 347g | — |

Result: identical to UK double cream (50% fat). Whips in seconds by hand. Doesn't separate. Lasts long in fridge. Works with any cream type (pasteurized, ultra-pasteurized).

### Custom Milk/Cream from Scratch

Use highest-fat cream available + cold water + SMP to create any fat% dairy product. SMP brings milk solids (protein + lactose) back in line. Lactose is water-soluble — only exists in the water element. The higher fat you go in cream, the less SMP you need (less water = less dilution of solids). This is reverse-engineering what dairies do.

### Cream Additives (Canada Problem)

Canadian creams are loaded with stabilizers (cellulose gel, carrageenan, cellulose gum) before you open the carton. These interfere with recipe balancing — when your cream already has 3 stabilizers, adding your own creates over-stabilization.

**Best options (Canada):**
1. Lucerne brand (only carrageenan — acceptable since it's a stabilizer you'd add anyway)
2. Dairyland 18% coffee cream (only sodium citrate)
3. Make your own cream via evaporation

**Global cream fat% reference:**
| Region | Product | Fat% |
|--------|---------|------|
| UK | Double Cream | 50% |
| UK | Single Cream | ~18% |
| Canada | Whipping Cream | 33% |
| US | Heavy Whipping Cream | 36-40% |

UK/European dairy is the best in the world for ice cream. UK double cream: 50% fat, perfect consistency, very cheap (~$0.65 CAD per 100g).

## No-Churn Ice Cream

Heavy/whipping cream + **sweetened** condensed milk (NOT evaporated milk — completely different product). The sugar in condensed milk is essential — without it, you get a solid block of ice.

**Target for decent no-churn: ~20-23% fat AND ~20-23% sugar.** This gives scoopability, decent taste, reasonable texture.
- 15% fat / 10% sugar = solid rock
- 28% sugar = barely freezes (only works with commercial blast chillers)

**How it works scientifically:**
- Fat prevents ice crystal enlargement
- Sugar reduces freezing point
- MSNF helps with overrun when whipping

**Tip:** Whip cream and condensed milk together (not cream alone first) — better texture. Aim for 15-20% volume increase by hand whipping.

**Biggest downside:** Condensed milk has a strong distinctive flavor that muddies all other flavors. Never as good as properly churned. Even a cheap $15-20 ice cream machine produces better results.

**Base recipe:** 400g whipping cream (33%) + 400g condensed milk (one 10oz can) + vanilla extract

## Equipment & Resources

### Five Essential Items

1. **Skim Milk Powder (SMP)** — NOT whole milk powder (weird taste). Increases solids. Mimics what pasteurization does (heating = evaporation = higher solids). Any supermarket
2. **Invert sugar** — corn syrup, glucose syrup, honey, maple syrup, golden syrup. Creates unique texture impossible to mimic without it
3. **Dextrose** — reduces freezing point for scoopability. Essential for Ninja Creami. Amazon / brewery shops, ~2kg bag, very cheap
4. **Thermometer** — any type. Evaporation starts at ~55C — impossible to gauge by eye. Amazon, ~$10
5. **Digital scales** — standard for ingredients + jeweler/micro scales for stabilizers (<$10). Cups are inadequate for ice cream science
6. **Bonus: Guar gum** — creates freezer longevity. Amazon. "Not as good" = xanthan gum

### Ice Cream Calculators (Ranked)

1. **Patrick Svenson's icecreamcalc.com** — the best. Only one that handles physical chocolate input. Fully customizable graphs, custom bars, freezing/serving temperature display. Harder to use
2. **Dream Scoops** — free, online, no download. Easier for beginners. Good for cocoa powder recipes. Set your actual milk/cream fat% at the top before anything else
3. **Scoopulator (scoopulator.app)** — newer. Target profiles, relative sweetness, custom ingredients. Good for formulation-first thinking

### Best Ice Cream Books (Ranked)

**Recommended:**
1. **"Hello, My Name Is Ice Cream" (Dana Cree)** — #1 pick. Deep dive into ice cream science. Explains what ingredients do and why. Best starting book
2. **"Ample Hills Creamery"** — excellent recipes, good flavors, solid science
3. **"Salt & Straw Ice Cream Cookbook"** — decent, creative unique flavors
4. **"Jude's Ice Cream & Desserts"** — good variety, all-rounder
5. **"Van Leeuwen Artisan Ice Cream"** — creative flavors, heavy vegan section
6. **"The Perfect Scoop" (David Lebovitz)** — shallow science dive, OK for reference

**Avoid:**
- **Ben & Jerry's book** — single base modified slightly. Claims theory but teaches nothing. Condensed milk bases = inferior results. Good for cooking with kids only
- **"Gelato Messina: The Recipes"** — "shockingly bad" for home makers. Recipes don't translate

**Pet hate:** Condensed milk in ice cream. Strong flavor muddies everything. It's functional but far from luxury quality. Recipe books that don't explain WHY behind ingredients — just lists without function.

### Classic Blends

| Blend | Ratio | Notes |
|-------|-------|-------|
| Commercial standard | LBG : Guar : Lambda Carrageenan = **4:2:1** | Most common commercial blend |
| With emulsifier | Lecithin : LBG : Guar : Lambda = **4:4:2:1** | Full stabilizer + emulsifier system |
| Simple cold-process | Gelatin + Xanthan | Gelatin for freeze state, xanthan for melt state |
| Budget commercial | CMC + GMS (60:30) + other | Cheap, but strong taste |

### Key Warnings

1. **Do NOT use whole milk powder** — weird taste, changes flavor. Use SMP only
2. **Do NOT add butter to cream** — won't re-emulsify, disgusting slimy mouthfeel
3. **Do NOT use drinking chocolate as cocoa powder** — mostly sugar, impossible to balance
4. **Do NOT boil cream during reduction** — scalding changes taste permanently
5. **Do NOT skip weighing** — cups are inadequate for ice cream science
6. **Do NOT substitute erythritol 1:1 for sugar** — won't freeze, recrystallizes, ultra-cold
7. **Black cocoa tastes disgusting** unless heavily boiled/cooked
8. **Pre-stabilized cream** (Canadian-style) interferes with your own recipe stabilizers
9. **Too much protein** = fudge-thickness base that won't churn
10. **Too much stabilizer** (even 0.5g over) = gummy, slimy, horrible texture
