# Microcrystalline Cellulose (MCC / E460i) — Ingredient Reference

> Researched 2026-03-13. All claims sourced from regulatory bodies, peer-reviewed papers, or manufacturer data.

---

## What Is It?

Purified, partially depolymerized cellulose — the same beta-1,4-linked glucose polymer in every plant cell wall, but with amorphous regions removed via acid hydrolysis, leaving highly crystalline microcrystals. Fine white odorless powder.

- **E-number:** E460(i). E460(ii) is powdered cellulose (less refined).
- **Other label names:** Cellulose gel, MCC, microcrystalline cellulose.
- **Made from:** Wood pulp or cotton linters, treated with hydrochloric acid.
- **Calories:** 0 kcal/g — not digested, not absorbed.
- **PAC:** ~0 (insoluble, does not dissolve, no freezing point depression).
- **POD:** 0 (no sweetness).

## Regulatory Status

### FDA (United States)
- **GRAS** under 21 CFR 184.1138 (direct food substance).
- Approved functions: anticaking, drying, flavor enhancer, formulation aid, humectant, stabilizer/thickener.
- **No quantity limit** — governed by GMP (Good Manufacturing Practice).
- Source: [FDA Substances Added to Food](https://hfpappexternal.fda.gov/scripts/fdcc/index.cfm?set=FoodSubstances&id=CELLULOSEMICROCRYSTALLINE)

### EFSA (European Union)
- Authorized as E460(i) under Commission Regulation (EU) No 231/2012.
- **2018 re-evaluation** (ANS Panel): "No need for a numerical ADI" and "no safety concern at the reported uses and use levels."
- Indicative safe exposure: ~660-900 mg/kg body weight/day (~46-63g/day for a 70kg person).
- Source: [EFSA 2018 (PMC7009359)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7009359/)
- Source: [EFSA 2017 Amendment (PMC7010068)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7010068/)

### JECFA (WHO/FAO)
- ADI: **"Not specified"** — most favorable category, no safety concern at any reasonable dietary level.
- Source: [JECFA Monograph, WHO Food Additives Series 40](https://www.inchem.org/documents/jecfa/jecmono/v040je03.htm)

## Health & Safety

### Gut Fermentation — The Key Finding

**MCC is essentially NOT fermented in the human gut.**

| Fiber | Gut Fermentation | Gas/Bloating Risk |
|-------|:----------------:|:-----------------:|
| Inulin | ~100% (rapid, complete) | High above 15-20g |
| Xylitol | Significant (dose-dependent) | Moderate above 20-30g |
| **MCC** | **~5-7.6%** (minimal) | **Essentially none** |

- **In vitro:** Only 5-7.6% fermentation in 24h batch fermentation with human fecal microbiota.
  Source: [PMC10000603](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10000603/)
- **In vivo (human):** 98.9 +/- 3.0% of radiolabeled MCC recovered in feces within 2 days.
  Source: [JECFA Monograph](https://www.inchem.org/documents/jecfa/jecmono/v040je03.htm)
- **In vivo (rat):** 96-104% fecal recovery at 10-20% of diet, no urinary radioactivity.
  Source: [JECFA Monograph](https://www.inchem.org/documents/jecfa/jecmono/v040je03.htm)

Reason: MCC's high crystallinity makes it inaccessible to colonic microbial enzymes. A small subset of methane-excreting individuals may have bacteria (Ruminococcus champanellensis) capable of partial degradation.
Source: [Oxford Academic FEMS (2003)](https://academic.oup.com/femsec/article/46/1/81/471388)

### Digestibility
Not digested. Not absorbed. Passes through the entire GI tract unchanged. Zero metabolizable energy.

### Side Effects
- **At food-use levels (3-15g):** No adverse effects expected per EFSA and JECFA.
- **At high doses:** Increased fecal bulk (expected for insoluble fiber), occasional mild laxation from large bolus. No systemic toxicity in 90-day rat studies at up to 50,000 mg/kg diet.
- **At extreme levels:** 30% of diet (15,000 mg/kg BW) for 72 weeks in rats — no effect on survival, feed efficiency, or hematology.

### Toxicology
- **No genotoxicity, no carcinogenicity, no reproductive toxicity** in any study reviewed by EFSA or JECFA.
- **Persorption concern:** JECFA noted a theoretical concern that very small particles (<5 micrometer) could pass through the intestinal wall. This led to specification limits on particle size. EFSA's 2018 re-evaluation acknowledged this but maintained "no safety concern."
- **Nutrient absorption:** No peer-reviewed evidence of nutrient binding at food-use levels. Not flagged by EFSA or JECFA.

## Use in Ice Cream

### Commercial Products Containing MCC
MCC (listed as "cellulose gel") is standard in commercial low-cal/reduced-fat ice cream:

- **Halo Top** — chocolate, vanilla bean, strawberry, and other flavors
  Source: [halotop.com/pints/chocolate](https://halotop.com/pints/chocolate)
- **Enlightened** — "cellulose gel and gum" in formulations
- **McDonald's** reduced-fat ice cream products (sundaes, McFlurry, shakes)

### Functions in Ice Cream
1. **Fat mimetic:** MCC particles in suspension mimic fat globule mouthfeel. A patent (EP1937080A2) shows 0.4-1.0% colloidal MCC in 5% butterfat ice cream makes it taste like 10% butterfat.
   Source: [EP1937080A2](https://patents.google.com/patent/EP1937080A2/en)
2. **Ice crystal inhibitor:** Immobilizes water molecules, preventing crystal growth during storage and heat shock.
3. **Foam/texture stabilizer:** Preserves air cell structure (overrun).
4. **Bulking agent:** Adds volume without calories.

### Typical Dosage
- **Colloidal MCC systems:** 0.3-1.6% of finished ice cream, typically 1.0-1.4%
- **As fat replacer:** 0.2-1.0%, typically 0.4-0.9%
- **In a 680g Creami pint:** 1% = 6.8g. Practical range: **3-10g per pint.**

### Colloidal vs. Plain MCC
**Important:** For ice cream, colloidal MCC (co-processed with CMC or a gum) works much better than plain powder. Plain MCC doesn't hydrate/disperse well in cold systems.

Commercial colloidal MCC products: Avicel (FMC/DuPont), MICROCEL (Roquette).

**Workaround for plain MCC (what we'd use):**
- Combine plain MCC powder with a small amount of CMC (already in our pantry via Salty Stability).
- Blend thoroughly with immersion blender — MCC needs shear to disperse.
- The Creami spin cycle provides additional shear during processing.

## Use in Other Foods

MCC is extremely common in processed food:
- **Shredded/grated cheese** — anticaking agent
- **Low-calorie/diet foods** — bulking, fat replacement
- **Fat-free salad dressings** — texture/body
- **Whipped toppings** — foam stability
- **Baked goods** — moisture retention
- **Sauces and condiments** — thickening
- **Dietary supplements/tablets** — binder/filler (most common use overall)

Source: [Today.com — Cellulose in shredded cheese](https://www.today.com/health/diet-fitness/cellulose-powder-shredded-cheese-rcna160695)

## MCC vs. Inulin for Our Recipes

| Property | Inulin | MCC |
|----------|:------:|:---:|
| Gut fermentation | ~100% | ~5-7% |
| GI risk | High >15-20g | Essentially none |
| Calories | 1.5 kcal/g | 0 |
| PAC | 0.22 | ~0 |
| POD (sweetness) | 8% of sugar | 0 |
| Body mechanism | Dissolves, viscosity/gel | Suspended particles, fat mimetic |
| Ice crystal prevention | Indirect (water binding) | Direct (water immobilization) |
| Solubility | Soluble | Insoluble (suspension) |
| Ease of use | Dissolves easily | Needs blending; best with CMC |
| Scoopulator modeling | Yes (dissolved solid) | No (insoluble, not modeled) |

**MCC is NOT a 1:1 inulin replacement.** They work via different mechanisms. Use MCC for body/texture/ice-crystal roles. Keep erythritol/xylitol/glycerin for PAC (scoopability). If any inulin remains in the recipe (e.g., inside SS), it still contributes its PAC and solids.

## Honest Unknowns

- Long-term daily consumption at 10-20g in ice cream specifically has not been studied in human clinical trials (data is from animal studies and general exposure estimates).
- Interaction between MCC and our specific ingredient stack (whey, erythritol, xylitol, glycerin) in the gut has not been specifically studied. However, since MCC is inert and passes through undigested, interactions are unlikely.
- How well plain (non-colloidal) MCC performs in a Creami context needs real-world testing. The immersion blender + Creami spin should provide enough shear, but we won't know until we try.
