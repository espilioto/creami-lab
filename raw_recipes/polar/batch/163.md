### [Upgrade Your Desserts with Homemade Vanilla Paste](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OSFnAc0NhN8)

Technique/component video — how to make a rich, long-lasting vanilla bean paste from scratch using a sugar syrup base.

**Ingredients:**

- **70g** vanilla beans (Grade B or better — ~17 beans; must still be pliable, not bone-dry)
- **280g** water
- **160g** sugar
- **50g** corn syrup

**Steps:**

1. Sterilize your storage jar or bottle thoroughly.
2. Combine water, sugar, and corn syrup in a saucepan. Place on medium-low heat and stir to dissolve.
3. Trim the hard ends off the vanilla beans, then split each bean lengthwise with a small knife.
4. Chop the split beans into ~5mm pieces (seeds and pod together — don't discard the seeds).
5. Remove the syrup from heat briefly, add the chopped vanilla beans, stir, then return to medium-low heat.
6. Simmer for ~45 minutes, keeping a gentle bubble, until the syrup has reduced and thickened.
7. Allow to cool sufficiently before blending (hot liquid expands and can overflow a blender).
8. Blend or process the entire mixture (beans + syrup) until smooth. A large food processor works best; a smoothie blender also works — blend for a good while.
9. Strain through a sieve into the sterilized jar, pushing through as much liquid as possible from the bean solids.
10. Store in the refrigerator — the paste will thicken further when cold.

**Notes:**

- The corn syrup prevents recrystallization of the sugar syrup; honey can substitute (it's a natural invert sugar) but adds its own flavor.
- Grade A beans are ideal but expensive; Grade B works well. Avoid completely dried-out beans with no flexibility — minimal flavor remains.
- Do not discard the strained bean solids — dry them in the oven and use in other recipes.
- Vanilla bean paste keeps for a long time refrigerated; vanilla flavor compounds are stable.
- Vanilla acts as a flavor booster (like salt) — use 1/4 tsp in hot chocolate or cocoa, or add to savory dishes.
- This recipe makes a large batch (~500g paste); scale down freely (half, quarter, etc.).
- Use a large food processor, not a small countertop mixer — heat retained in the mix can cause overflow in a small machine.
